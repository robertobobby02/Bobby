# First Contact

## Scene: Private Park arrives at the 7th's forward post

---

The transport's ramp hit dirt and Private Park walked into the worst smell of her life.

It wasn't rot. Rot she knew. This was something chemical and alive at the same time, like someone had boiled engine coolant in a terrarium. It coated the back of her throat and stayed there.

"You get used to it," said the corporal who'd drawn escort duty. He didn't look at her when he said it, which meant he was lying.

The forward post was smaller than she'd expected. Prefab barracks arranged in a loose square, a motor pool, a mess tent with one wall sagging. Everything had that look — the look of a place that had been built to last six weeks and was now on month nine. Duct tape on the generator housing. Bootprints worn into the ground so deep they held rainwater.

Park shouldered her duffel and followed the corporal past the mess tent toward a row of bunks along the eastern wall. Marines were scattered around in the usual configurations: cleaning weapons, sleeping with their mouths open, arguing about nothing. Nobody looked up. New boots weren't interesting.

Then she saw the tail.

It came around the corner of the motor pool first — black, bladed, longer than a car. It moved with a lazy drag, the tip carving a line in the dirt the way someone might trail their fingers through sand. The rest of him followed.

Park stopped walking.

She had read the briefing. She had watched the footage. She had sat through a forty-minute orientation video narrated by a woman whose entire job seemed to be making nightmare fuel sound administrative. None of it had prepared her for the actual size of him.

Twenty-five feet. The number had been in the file. But twenty-five feet in a file is just a number, and twenty-five feet in front of you is a wall of black chitin that blocks out the sky. His dome caught the light and threw it back slick and wrong, like oil on water. No eyes. She knew there were no eyes. It was worse in person because his head still *tracked* — it moved with a purpose that said he knew exactly where she was, exactly what she was doing, probably what she'd eaten for breakfast. Dorsal tubes rose off his back in a curved row, and thin smoke threaded out of them and curled upward in the still air.

He was holding a cigar. Between two claws, delicate as a pencil in a fist. He brought it to his mouth — and she watched the inner jaw extend just far enough to grip it, pull, and retract. Smoke came out of his dorsal tubes in four lazy streams.

"That's Adam," said the corporal, still walking.

Park was not still walking. Park was standing very still in the middle of the path with her duffel sliding off her shoulder.

A marine sitting on an ammo crate near Adam's feet glanced over. "New boot?"

"Yeah," the corporal said.

The marine looked at Park. Looked at Adam. Looked back at Park.

"She's doing the face," he said.

"They all do the face," the corporal said.

Adam's head turned toward her. The dome angled down. Twenty-five feet of xenomorph king regarded Private Park with no eyes and total attention, and Park's hindbrain screamed a single clear note that translated roughly to *run run run run run —*

**You gonna stand there all day or do you need someone to carry your shit.**

Park flinched so hard she bit her tongue.

The voice — it wasn't a voice. It was in her head. Not like a thought. Like someone had opened a door in her skull that she didn't know was there and walked in without knocking. The words landed heavy, physical almost, the way bass from a speaker hits your chest before you hear it. And the voice itself was flat. Bored. The voice of a sergeant who'd said the same thing to a hundred new boots and wasn't going to make it interesting for the hundred-and-first.

"I—" Park started, out loud, and then didn't know if she was supposed to talk out loud. She looked at the corporal. The corporal was already ten feet ahead and clearly not coming back.

**Out loud's fine. I'm not picky.**

"Yes, sir. I mean — I'm good. I have my — I have it." She picked up her duffel. She had not noticed it hit the ground.

The marine on the ammo crate was eating something out of a bag. He didn't look up. "She called him sir."

**Yeah I heard.**

"They always do that."

**Makes me feel old.**

"You are old."

Adam didn't respond to that. He took another pull on the cigar. The smoke drifted out of his tubes and Park watched it rise because watching the smoke was easier than watching any other part of him. His tail swept behind him, slow, and the bladed tip passed close enough to the ammo crate marine that it ruffled the guy's hair. The marine didn't flinch. Didn't even look.

Park stood there.

She couldn't figure out where to put him. Every part of her training said *xenomorph* — the profile, the dome, the tail, the jaw — and every part of the scene said *Tuesday*. Marines weren't tense. Marines weren't armed-up and alert. A marine was eating chips six inches from a bladed tail that could gut a man faster than he could blink, and his biggest concern was getting crumbs in the bag.

"Where's my bunk?" Park asked, because it was the only sentence she could find.

The corporal, who had finally noticed she wasn't behind him, pointed at the barracks. "Third row, any open rack. Head's on the left. Don't use the second shower, it bites."

"Bites?"

"The faucet's stripped. It'll spin and catch your hand."

"Oh." Park had been expecting a worse answer.

She walked to the barracks. She did not walk past Adam. She walked *around* Adam, giving him a berth so wide she almost clipped the mess tent. She did not look back to see if he noticed. She knew he noticed. The thing about something with no eyes is you can't tell yourself it isn't watching.

---

She found a rack. Third row, upper bunk, mattress that was more suggestion than object. She stowed her duffel, sat on the edge, and stared at the wall.

Her tongue still hurt where she'd bitten it.

The orientation video had covered telepathy. "Shepherd-class xenomorphs may communicate through directed neural projection," the narrator had said, like she was reading a spec sheet. "Recipients report the experience as an interior voice, distinct from their own thoughts. There is no evidence of involuntary access to recipient cognition." Which was a very polished way of saying *he talks in your head but he's not reading your mind.* Park was not reassured. The difference between *can't* and *doesn't* felt pretty thin when you were standing in front of something that didn't have a face.

She unpacked. Weapon under the rack, kit at the foot, toiletries in the mesh pocket on the wall. She did all of it slowly because doing it slowly meant she didn't have to go back outside where Adam was.

She went back outside because she was hungry and the MREs were in the mess.

Adam had moved. He was on the far side of the compound now, crouched near the perimeter with two marines who were looking at a tablet. His tail was curled around behind him, the tip flicking. From this distance he looked like a piece of heavy equipment someone had parked wrong. One of the marines said something and Adam's head dipped — a nod, Park realized. He nodded. Of course he nodded. He had a human mind, the file said. Full cognition, full language, full emotional range. He just also had an exoskeleton and a pharyngeal jaw and enough mass to roll a transport.

She got her MRE. Beef stew, which meant brown paste that had once been in the same room as a cow. She sat on a bench near the mess tent and ate and watched him from across the compound and tried to make the two halves of it fit together: the shape that was every nightmare she'd been trained to kill, and the way the marines around him leaned in instead of away.

One of the marines at the tablet laughed. Park couldn't hear what was said. She watched Adam's tail tip flick once, fast — and she thought, maybe, that was the joke. That the tail was the punchline.

She had no idea what to do with that.

---

Night was worse.

The barracks were dark and full of the sounds of marines sleeping — breathing, shifting, someone grinding their teeth three racks down. Park lay on her back and stared at the ceiling and could not stop thinking about the fact that he was out there.

She didn't know where he slept. Did he sleep? The briefing hadn't covered it. She imagined him standing in the dark somewhere at the edge of the compound, motionless, that blind dome pointed at nothing, and she could not make herself stop imagining it.

The smell was worse at night. Thicker. She pulled her blanket up and breathed through the fabric and it didn't help.

She heard something.

Low. So low it was almost below hearing — more vibration than sound. It came through the floor of the barracks, up through the frame of her rack, into the mattress and into her spine. A rumble. Steady, rhythmic, continuous. Not mechanical. Not a generator. Organic.

Park lay very still and listened to it.

It was — she didn't have a word for it. It was the sound a big cat might make if big cats were twenty-five feet long and made of chitin and acid. A purr. It resonated in her ribs and in the backs of her teeth and somewhere behind her sternum that she couldn't name.

Around her, marines slept.

They slept like people sleep when they're safe. Not the light, one-ear-open sleep of a forward post in hostile territory. Real sleep. Deep sleep. The corporal was snoring. The teeth-grinder had stopped grinding.

The purr did that, Park realized. The purr was doing that. It rolled through the barracks like a tide and the marines sank into it without knowing, without caring, the way you sink into a warm current and stop swimming.

Park did not sink.

Park lay rigid on her rack with her fingers curled into her blanket and her heart beating too fast and the purr moving through her like something trying to find a door she wouldn't unlock. It wasn't hostile. She knew that. She *knew* that. It was the opposite of hostile — it was the most nonhostile thing she had ever felt, and that was exactly the problem. Because her body didn't know what to do with safety that came from something shaped like that. Twenty-five feet. Black dome. Inner jaw. Her training said *threat* and the purr said *no* and she was caught between them like a coin spinning on its edge.

She didn't sleep.

She lay there and listened to the purr and watched the dark and told herself all the things the briefing said: *he's an asset, he's stable, he's been with the 7th for years, no incidents, no casualties, full cognitive function, human mind, human loyalties —*

The purr dropped lower. She felt it in her hip bones.

In the rack below her, a marine mumbled something and rolled over and settled deeper into his pillow with a long exhale, like a kid who'd just been told everything's fine.

Park stared at the ceiling. Her eyes were wet and she didn't know why.

She was still scared. She was going to be scared for a while. But lying in the dark with the purr in her bones, she understood something the briefing hadn't covered and the orientation video hadn't mentioned and nobody had told her because it was the kind of thing you couldn't tell someone, you could only lie in the dark and find it:

He was keeping watch.

Not like a sentry. Not like a guard dog. Like something bigger and older and stranger than either of those, something that had wrapped itself around this handful of sleeping humans and drawn a line in the dark and said *mine.*

Park did not sleep. But at some point, very late, she closed her eyes.

The purr held.
